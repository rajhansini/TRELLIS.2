Airplane blub

Effect is good, but shape already used. Can you do it on a car? Can you try rust effect on car/motorcycle?
Mesh ready, prompt written. Needs one Kling video, then a 30-epoch fit.

The mesh was already solved: best_view.py records car at up −Z, yaw 70, pitch 30, 31.1% of its surface exposed, and the render was sitting in front/car.png. No re-render was needed.

Rust is a good call for a second reason. It answers the “looks like texture optimization” objection raised elsewhere in this review, because on a vehicle rust has a causal spatial order a viewer already knows: it starts where water sits and paint is broken — panel seams, lower door edges, wheel-arch lips, stone chips on the sills — and bleeds outward and upward. That is the opposite of uniform refinement. The prompt enforces it with an explicit ordering block, including “the roof is the last surface to be touched” and “no area cleans, brightens, lifts or reverses”.

Check before fitting. The render shows white slivers on the hood, roof and flank. Those are holes or thin shells in the mesh, not shading. Kling will paint into them, so inspect the returned video for pattern showing through the body before committing a fit. bike.obj and truck.obj are in the same folder if the motorcycle variant is preferred.

Blub drying

Result is ok, but looks like a texture optimization process.
Not fixable by frame selection. Needs a new reference video with an ordering constraint.

This one is diagnosable rather than a matter of taste. The reference video’s large frame-to-frame changes are clustered at frames 12–33 and then it goes quiet: the effect arrives almost all at once near the start and merely refines afterwards. That is exactly the signature being objected to — no nucleation order, no advancing front, just convergence, which is what an optimiser looks like.

The fix is the same ordering lock now written for the bunny and the car: name where the effect must begin and why, forbid any region from running ahead of its neighbour, and forbid anything from reversing.

The screenshot is diagA (yaw 45). Rotating 180° lands on diagC (yaw 225), where the crumpled leaf sits on the far side and leaves frame entirely, while the bud stays prominent at top-left.

train (yaw 0) is cleaner still, but it is the supervised view. diagC is unseen, which is what a comparison figure needs, so it is the better scientific choice as well as the requested one.

One caveat: at diagC a dark slit reads on the body’s right flank. That is the mouth, not damage. If it distracts, train is the fallback.

Method. Break points were found by measuring each reference video’s per-frame change against its own steady rate, then confirming visually against the rendered sequence. A first attempt measured PSNR of our render against the target instead, which drifts down monotonically and never flags a break — the dissolve originates in the reference, which our result then follows.
