\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:metrics}

\paragraph{Notation.}
For an object with mesh $M$ and a driving sequence of $T$ frames, the generator emits
one PBR voxel field per frame. Write $C_t \in [0,1]^{V \times 3}$ for the
\emph{base-colour} channels of the decoded field at frame $t$, over the $V$ occupied
voxels, obtained from the decoder output $\mathcal{D}(z_t)$ as
$C_t = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\mathcal{D}(z_t)_{0:3} + 1\big)$. Write $\hat{I}_t$ for the
image rendered from that field through the supervised camera and $I_t$ for the
corresponding target frame.

\subsection{Temporal metrics in texel space}

All three temporal quantities are computed on $C_t$ --- the field the model
predicts --- and not on rendered pixels. Two properties follow, and both are the
reason the numbers are meaningful. First, they are \emph{view-independent}: a
pixel-space temporal metric confounds texture change with camera motion and can
therefore only be read at a fixed camera, whereas a texel metric is defined on the
object and a single number covers the whole surface, including regions no evaluation
camera ever sees. Second, they observe the quantity the method actually produces;
rendering is a lossy projection, and shading, occlusion and rasterisation each
contribute their own frame-to-frame variation.

The definitions presuppose that consecutive frames describe the \emph{same} voxels.
The implementation asserts this per frame ($\texttt{torch.equal}$ on the coordinate
tensor); without it, a difference between frames could be comparing distinct points
in space. Let $\langle \cdot \rangle$ denote the mean over all $3V$ entries of a
$V \times 3$ matrix.

\paragraph{Texel flicker} is the mean first temporal difference,
\begin{equation}
  F \;=\; \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}
          \Big\langle \big| C_t - C_{t-1} \big| \Big\rangle ,
  \qquad
  \big\langle X \big\rangle \;=\; \frac{1}{3V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\sum_{c=1}^{3} X_{vc}.
  \label{eq:flicker}
\end{equation}
It measures how much the surface changes from one frame to the next. It is a
\emph{rate}: a texture that evolves quickly has high flicker whether or not it does
so smoothly, so $F$ alone cannot be read as a quality score.

\paragraph{Texel acceleration} is the mean second temporal difference,
\begin{equation}
  A \;=\; \frac{1}{T-2}\sum_{t=3}^{T}
          \Big\langle \big| C_t - 2C_{t-1} + C_{t-2} \big| \Big\rangle .
  \label{eq:accel}
\end{equation}
It measures how abruptly that rate itself changes, and it is the quantity that
corresponds to what a viewer calls flickering: a texture evolving quickly but
\emph{steadily} has high $F$ and low $A$, whereas one that jitters back and forth has
high $A$ even when its net motion is small. $A$ is therefore the metric that separates
temporal coherence from temporal speed.

\paragraph{Texel drift} compares the first frame to the last,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta \;=\; \Big\langle \big| C_T - C_1 \big| \Big\rangle ,
  \label{eq:drift}
\end{equation}
and is reported as a \textbf{guard, not a score}. Both $F$ and $A$ are minimised by a
degenerate solution --- a texture that does not move at all --- and $\Delta$ detects
exactly that failure. It is meaningful only alongside the other two, no best value is
marked for it in any table, and a reduction in $F$ or $A$ counts only if $\Delta$ is
held.

\paragraph{On the name.} This second-difference quantity was called \emph{texel jerk}
in earlier internal results and in the field names of the released measurement files.
In kinematics \emph{jerk} is the third derivative, while \eqref{eq:accel} is the
second; we use \emph{texel acceleration} throughout, with \emph{temporal roughness} as
an acceptable plainer alternative.

\subsection{Fidelity metrics}

PSNR and SSIM are computed at the supervised camera, against a render of the same mesh
through the same camera, and are restricted to the \emph{intersection mask}
$\Omega_t = R_t \cap G_t$, where $R_t$ is the set of pixels our render covers and
$G_t$ the set the target covers. Because the target is a render of the same mesh, the
two silhouettes agree by construction; what $\Omega_t$ removes is the sliver where a
covered pixel's triangle carries an unsupervised vertex and therefore has no ground
truth colour. With $\hat{I}_t, I_t \in [0,1]^{H \times W \times 3}$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MSE}_t \;=\; \frac{1}{3|\Omega_t|}
    \sum_{p \in \Omega_t} \big\| \hat{I}_t(p) - I_t(p) \big\|_2^2 ,
  \qquad
  \mathrm{PSNR}_t \;=\; 10 \log_{10}\!\frac{1}{\mathrm{MSE}_t}.
  \label{eq:psnr}
\end{equation}
For SSIM we compute the full per-pixel SSIM map $S_t$ with a $7{\times}7$ uniform
window and $\mathrm{data\ range}=1$, then average it over the mask,
$\mathrm{SSIM}_t = |\Omega_t|^{-1}\sum_{p \in \Omega_t} S_t(p)$. Both are reported as
means over the $T$ frames.

\paragraph{Why masked.} The background occupies $89.4\%$ of the pixels and is
byte-identical white in prediction and target, so whole-image SSIM is dominated by it:
representative objects score $0.985 / 0.983 / 0.980$ over the whole image against
$0.887 / 0.871 / 0.841$ on the silhouette. \texttt{skimage} provides no masked SSIM
estimator, so we take the full map from the same estimator and average it over honest
support.

\paragraph{Scope.} PSNR and SSIM are measured at the supervised view only, whereas
$F$, $A$ and $\Delta$ are view-independent by construction. PSNR also cannot settle a
temporal question on its own: it scores each frame against its own target
independently and is structurally blind to coherence, so two sequences with identical
per-frame error --- one smooth, one alternating --- receive the same PSNR. The
temporal metrics carry the claim; PSNR and SSIM are reported alongside to show that
fidelity is not traded for coherence.

\subsection{Temporal conditioning}

The parameter-free temporal component blends each frame's image-conditioning tokens
over a short window before the flow model sees them, so neighbouring frames share
conditioning and cannot drift apart independently. Let $c_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$
be frame $t$'s conditioning tokens ($N = 1029$, $d = 1024$) and
$\mathcal{W} = \{-1, 0, +1\}$ the window, clamped at the sequence ends. For each token
index $i$ independently,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{c}_t[i] \;=\; \sum_{o \in \mathcal{W}} \alpha_o \, c_{t+o}[i],
  \qquad
  \alpha \;=\; \mathrm{softmax}_{o \in \mathcal{W}}
    \Big( \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\, \langle c_t[i],\, c_{t+o}[i] \rangle \Big).
  \label{eq:mcfm}
\end{equation}
Token $i$ attends only over token $i$ in neighbouring frames; spatial mixing is left to
the model's own attention downstream. Equation~\eqref{eq:mcfm} introduces no learned
parameters.

\subsection{Training objective}

Adapters are trained with a masked reconstruction term and a perceptual term,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} \;=\; \frac{1}{3|\Omega_t|}\sum_{p \in \Omega_t}
      \big\| \hat{I}_t(p) - I_t(p) \big\|_1
    \;+\; \lambda \, \mathrm{LPIPS}\big(\hat{I}_t, I_t\big),
  \qquad \lambda = 0.1,
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
with LPIPS on a frozen AlexNet backbone, evaluated on the tight bounding-box crop of
the silhouette at native resolution --- neither clamped nor downsampled, since
clamping removes the gradient on out-of-range pixels and downsampling discards the
high frequencies the term exists to restore.

\subsection{Aggregation and significance}

Each table row may average several arms. We first average a row's arms
\emph{within} each object, then average over objects, so that a row backed by ten arms
does not outvote one backed by a single arm. Reported significance is a two-sided sign
test over the $24$ objects, counting the objects on which one row beats another;
because it is computed over objects rather than seeds, it speaks to consistency across
the object set and not to seed variance.
